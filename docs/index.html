Methods

Coral reefs are being rapidly degraded under climate change. This project estimates the impact of coral cover declines on economic benefits of nature associated with coral reefs attributable to tourism, coastal protection, and fisheries.

This page documents data and methodologies employed in the impact accounting throughout the dashboard.

Orlando Timmerman

PhD researcher, Department of Earth Sciences, University of Cambridge

Personal site · University profile

Note: It is the author's opinion that nature is invaluable. It is not a commodity that can be bought, sold, or traded. Peoples' connection to nature is not quantifiable. The purpose of this project is to speak to decision- and policy-makers to show them the impact of inaction in language and numbers that matter to them. I hope that this will add to the countless voices clamouring for faster, more equitable, and more meaningful action to curb emissions and protect these priceless ecosystems.

1. Analytical framework

The pipeline has four main stages:

  1. Project coral cover at sites across the world under RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5 for 2050 and 2100.
  2. Assign baseline economic value to each reef site or country for tourism, coastal protection, and fisheries.
  3. Align projections of coral cover to economics of local reefs via nearest-neighbour matching in geographic space.
  4. Apply sector-specific depreciation models to compute remaining value, annual loss, and cumulative opportunity cost.

Results are reported at site level (see Map) and aggregated to countries (see Country-level Impact).

2. Coral cover projections

Reef Check

Baseline coral cover at reference sites are taken from the Reef Check database. The Reef Check organisation dictates a standardised method of benthic cover (and fish life) assessment which is utilised by trained practitioners all over the world.

A benthic survey consists of four 20m transects separated by 5m gaps. Benthic cover is recorded at every 0.5 metres along the survey using the Reef Check classification schema. This includes (living) 'Hard Coral' i.e. scleractinia which form the basis of the structural complexity which forms reefs and provides many of its ecosystem services. For each transect, 'coral cover' is the percentage of points containing 'Hard Coral'.

Cover projection model

Future coral cover is modelled in response to various environmental covariates including past and present sea surface temperature, turbidity, and exposure to cyclones as well as proxies of local stressors such as proximate human population. A log-linear response function in a hierarchical Bayesian beta framework achieves reasonable R2 scores. The model's coefficients (and uncertainties) for each covariate are estimated using a Monte Carlo markov chain schema. 'Hierarchical' refers to the model's structure nesting observations at both site and ecoregion levels. This enables more accurate, while samples which have few (or no) repeat measurements are borrow from the characteristics of the other sites in the shared ecoregion.

This hierarchical model is similar to that employed in Sully et al. (2022), but employs features optimised for – and validated under – the task of projecting long-term changes in coral cover projection.

Coral cover trajectories between 2013 and each scenario endpoint (2050, 2100) are linearly and exponentially interpolated for for cumulative loss calculations, although this makes minimal difference to the resulting values.

Ecoregion-level random effects in the hierarchical model are informed by species richness from the Coral Reefs of the World (COTW) ecoregion classification. Each monitoring site is assigned to a COTW polygon; total scleractinian species counts per ecoregion are z-scored and included as a predictor of ecoregion intercepts (diversity.standardized in the model).

Historical sea surface temperatures

The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 6 (CMIP6) hosts hindcast and forecast projections of various ocean variables. Historic sea surface temperature (SST) proxies organisms' evolutionary history which may include adaptation to present-day (and future, including higher) temperatures. Global SSTs from five Earth system models identified by X et al. (2022) as being particularly good at describing conditions on the worlds' reefs were downloaded from ESGF Metagrid using the esgpull-plus package.

The 'historic' period was defined as the period before mass bleaching was observed (1850-1980). Nearest neighbour values of SST at Reef Check points over this period were extracted and mapped to the forecast sea surface temperatures (see below) via quantile distribution mapping (QDM). The resulting SST timeseries were parameterised by mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values.

CMIP6 / QDM SST summaries at reef monitoring locations (— points).

Recent sea surface temperatures

Recent thermal exposure is taken from NOAA Coral Reef Watch (CRW) products built on the CoralTemp sea surface temperature archive. At each Reef Check location, daily observed sea surface temperature anomaly (SSTA), hotspot, and degree heating week (DHW) time series are aggregated over a 1985–2014 reference climatology to give site-level summaries of mean anomaly, variability, peak thermal stress, and the frequency of severe bleaching years (annual DHW peaks ≥ 8 °C-weeks).

For each survey, an additional time-varying covariate records mean SST over the three years preceding the survey date. Where absolute SST is required, observed CRW SSTA is combined with a day-of-year climatology derived from QDM-bias-corrected CoralTemp at the nearest reef location.

NOAA CRW / CoralTemp stress metrics aggregated over 1985–2014 at reef locations.

Forecast sea surface temperatures

Future SSTs come from the same CMIP6 ensemble used for historic hindcasts. For each model and Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5), daily forecast SST (2015–2100) is extracted at reef locations, bias-corrected with quantile distribution mapping against the 1985–2014 reference, and stitched onto the corrected historic series. At each site, the per-model mean SST over each forecast window is summarised by the ensemble mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum.

Turbidity

Turbidity is proxied by the diffuse attenuation coefficient at 490 nm (Kd490) from NASA's MODIS-Aqua mapped monthly ocean-colour product (4 km). Monthly values at each reef location are converted to linear m⁻¹, capped at 1.0 following Sully et al., and summarised over 2003–2014 as mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum turbidity.

MODIS-Aqua Kd490 summaries (2003–2014) at reef locations.

Cyclone exposure

Cyclone exposure is derived from the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS) v04r01. Individual storm tracks are interpolated along each best-track segment, rasterised onto a 0.1° latitude/longitude grid, and converted to a mean annual storm-frequency surface (with light Gaussian smoothing). This value is sampled at each survey coordinate as a static proxy for chronic cyclone disturbance.

Global cyclone frequency surface (1° subsample) with reef-location values overlaid.

Population density

Coastal human population density is taken from WorldPop FuturePop 1 km rasters under the SSP2 socioeconomic pathway. For each Reef Check survey, the raster for the nearest available FuturePop year is sampled at the survey coordinates (people per km²), providing a time-varying proxy for local anthropogenic pressure on reefs.

3. Economic value datasets

The many economic benefits to humans which reefs provide can be divided broadly into three categories: on-reef and reef-adjacent tourism, infrastructure protected from extreme weather by reef structures, and income/value from reef-sustained fisheries.

The value and depdence on coral cover of each category is calculated independently before being aggregated by country:

Tourism

Reef tourism value is derived from spatially distributed willingness-to-pay / trip expenditure surfaces estimated by Spalding et al. (2017). Values are expressed in USD per year at each reef site.

Coastal protection

Reef structures provide protection for livelihoods and infrastructure along the coasts by dissipating wave energy. The financial value of infrastructure protected is taken from estimates by Burke and Spalding (2022). The spatial distribution of this value is taken from the accompanying dataset (provided by Ocean Wealth) and is expressed as USD spared per coastal grid cell.

Fisheries

Baseline values of fisheries are taken from the Sea Around Us public API (api.seaaroundus.org). The Sea Around Us combines official reported fish catch data over several decades from the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation's 'FishStat' database with reconstructed estimates of unreported data, and represents the best-available measure of global fish take and its associated economic value. Data is provided at the country level.

National fish take is disaggregated into various categories, including small, medium, and large reef associated fish as well as e.g. pelagics, sharks etc. While we only include the value of these reef associated fish, it is noted that fish which are not singularly associated with reefs, and those higher up the trophic pyramid are in many cases dependent on coral reefs for their early life stages and food. This study therefore likely describes a conservative value for reef associated fisheries.

For each country, the baseline fishery value is the average of the most recent decade for which data is available (2010-2019). The value of fisheries displayed on the Map shows a 0.1° raster of this value modified by the spatial in coral cover and weighted by the estimated fishing intensity from a 2021 Ocean Wealth report.

4. Spatial alignment

Each economic feature (reef polygon centroid or fisheries/coastal point) is matched to the nearest coral-cover monitoring site. Economic points beyond 5° from a reef survey site (X% of the economic datasets) are excluded from the analysis.

While the majority of the economic datasets (X%) lie within 1° (~111km) of a coral cover projection datapoint, the remaining X% for which Reef Check surveys are remote motivates additional targeted survey sampling in order to increase the representiveness of the data.

For smooth spatial visualisation on the Map without changing country aggregates, dense point layers are aggregated by mean onto a 0.1° grid.

5. Economic depreciation models

Having mapped economic value to reef surveys and projected coral cover at those points, we need relationships between cover change and value change — reflecting, for example, reefs becoming less attractive to tourists or providing less fish habitat as they degrade.

These models are deliberate – and necessary – oversimplifications. Regional, temporal, and cultural variation is large, but we apply one functional form per sector globally because finer-scaled estimates are not supported by the data. The results are intended as first-order, discussion-ready projections of how reef degradation may affect currently reef-derived economic services.

The interactive panels below show how each sector translates coral cover change into remaining economic value. Compound and tipping-point curves appear under tourism as cross-sector sensitivity scenarios.

Tourism — Chen et al. depreciation

Chen et al. (2015) tourism elasticity: 3.81% value loss per 1% relative coral loss (Vrem = V0 × max(0, 1 + 3.807 × ΔC/C0)). The compound and tipping-point panels are sensitivity scenarios applied identically across all sectors in the dashboard.

35%

Coastal protection — cover-proportional loss

Coastal protection value is assumed to scale 1:1 with live coral cover: at each site, the full baseline value is retained when cover equals the site baseline, and protection (and value) falls linearly to zero when cover reaches zero:

Vremaining = V0 ×   Cfinal
Cbaseline

This is a simple structural assumption that reef-mediated flood protection is proportional to remaining coral cover.

Fisheries — habitat quality model

Fisheries value scales with coral cover via a habitat-quality multiplier on Sea Around Us country baselines:

 Vremaining = V0 × m,

where m is the habitat quality multiplier; V0 is the initial value of the reef, and Vremaining is the value remaining after habitat degradation. m is defined as:

m = α + (1 − α) Cfinal
Cbaseline,

where α is the habitat floor: the minimum share of fisheries value retained when coral cover reaches zero. Cfinal and Cbaseline are the final and baseline coral covers respectively.

The dashboard uses α = 0.4 as indicated in Wilson et al. (2006); use the slider to explore the effect of other values of α on the value lost under as coral cover changes:

  • At α = 0, no fishery value remains at zero cover;
  • at α = 1, changes to cover have no effect on fishery value.

6. Cumulative impact over time

Endpoint losses (2050 / 2100) understate long-run harm because value is lost in every intervening year. We construct coral cover trajectories from a 2013 baseline to each scenario endpoint (linear or exponential interpolation), then compute year-by-year remaining value and opportunity cost.

  • Annual loss — year-over-year decline in remaining value.
  • Cumulative loss — sum of annual opportunity cost (V0 − Vt) discounted at 0% by default.
  • Tipping-point models are evaluated per site so reefs with higher C0 cross the collapse threshold later.

7. Country aggregation & GDP context

Site-level original_value, remaining_value, and value_loss are summed by country (ISO A3). Loss as a share of national GDP uses World Bank GDP (constant USD) for the reference year.

Country charts support annual endpoint loss and cumulative loss scenarios. The “all datasets” view sums tourism, coastal protection, and fisheries — note units differ by layer but aggregates are shown for comparative ranking.

8. Uncertainty, limitations, & next steps

Uncertainty quantification

The coral cover projections inherit structural and parameter uncertainty from the beta regression; RCP 8.5 is a high-emissions sensitivity, not a forecast.

The nearest-neighbour alignment can mismatch reef ecology and economic activity where monitoring sites are sparse.

The tourism elasticities follow Chen et al. (2015) meta-analysis; fisheries use SAU country baselines and a habitat floor — both are simplifying assumptions.
