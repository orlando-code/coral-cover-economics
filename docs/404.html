Methods

Coral reefs are being rapidly degraded under climate change. This project estimates the impact of coral cover declines on economic benefits of nature associated with coral reefs attributable to tourism, fisheries, and coastal protection.

This page documents data and methodologies employed in the impact accounting throughout the dashboard.

Note: It is the author's opinion that nature is invaluable. It is not a commodity that can be bought, sold, or traded. Peoples' connection to nature is not quantifiable. The purpose of this project is to speak to decision- and policy-makers to show them the impact of inaction in language and numbers that matter to them. I hope that this will add to the countless voices clamouring for faster, more equitable, and more meaningful action to curb emissions and protect these priceless ecosystems.

1. Analytical framework

The pipeline has four main stages:

  1. Project coral cover at sites across the world under RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5 for 2050 and 2100.
  2. Assign baseline economic value to each reef site or country for tourism, fisheries, and coastal protection.
  3. Align projections of coral cover to economics of local reefs via nearest-neighbour matching in geographic space.
  4. Apply sector-specific depreciation models to compute remaining value, annual loss, and cumulative opportunity cost.

Results are reported at site level (see Map) and aggregated to countries (see Country-level Impact).

2. Coral cover projections

Baseline coral cover at reference sites are taken from the Reef Check database. The Reef Check organisation dictates a standardised method of benthic cover (and fish life) assessment which is utilised by trained practitioners all over the world.

A benthic survey consists of four 20m transects separated by 5m gaps. Benthic cover is recorded at every 0.5 metres along the survey using the Reef Check classification schema. This includes (living) 'Hard Coral' i.e. scleractinia which form the basis of the structural complexity which forms reefs and provides many of its ecosystem services. For each transect, 'coral cover' is the percentage of points containing 'Hard Coral'. # TODO: reef check surveys over time and spatially

Future coral cover is modelled in response to various environmental covariates including past and present sea surface temperature, turbidity, and exposure to cyclones as well as proxies of local stressors such as proximate human population. A log-linear response function in a hierarchical Bayesian beta framework achieves reasonable R2 scores. The model's coefficients (and uncertainties) for each covariate are estimated using a Monte Carlo markov chain schema. 'Hierarchical' refers to the model's structure nesting observations at both site and ecoregion levels. This enables more accurate, while samples which have few (or no) repeat measurements are borrow from the characteristics of the other sites in the shared ecoregion. This hierarchical model is similar to that employed in Sully et al. (2022), but employs features optimised for – and validated under – the task of projecting long-term changes in coral cover projection. The author has also been in communication with Sully about statistical and technical issues with their model: a published erratum to the origianl paper is expected in the coming weeks. # TODO: forward forecasting methods # TODO: model diagram

Coral cover trajectories between 2013 and each scenario endpoint (2050, 2100) are linearly and exponentially interpolated for for cumulative loss calculations, although this makes minimal difference to the resulting values.

3. Economic value datasets

The many economic benefits to humans which reefs provide can be divided broadly into three categories: on-reef and reef-adjacent tourism, income/value from reef-sustained fisheries, and infrastructure protected from extreme weather by reef structures.

The value and depdence on coral cover of each category is calculated independently before being aggregated by country:

Tourism

Reef tourism value is derived from spatially distributed willingness-to-pay / trip expenditure surfaces estimated by Spalding et al. (2017). Values are expressed in USD per year at each reef site.

Fisheries

Baseline values of fisheries are taken from the Sea Around Us public API (api.seaaroundus.org). The Sea Around Us combines official reported fish catch data over several decades from the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation's 'FishStat' database with reconstructed estimates of unreported data, and represents the best-available measure of global fish take and its associated economic value. Data is provided at the country level.

National fish take is disaggregated into various categories, including small, medium, and large reef associated fish as well as e.g. pelagics, sharks etc. While we only include the value of these reef associated fish, it is noted that fish which are not singularly associated with reefs, and those higher up the trophic pyramid are in many cases dependent on coral reefs for their early life stages and food. This study therefore likely describes a conservative value for reef associated fisheries.

For each country, the baseline fishery value is the average of the most recent decade for which data is available (2010-2019). The value of fisheries displayed on the Mapshows a 0.1° raster of this value modified by the spatial in coral cover and weighted by the estimated fishing intensity from a 2021 Ocean Wealth report.

Coastal protection

Reef structures provide protection for livelihoods and infrastructure along the coasts by dissipating wave energy. The financial value of infrastructure protected is taken from estimates by Burke and Spalding (2022). The spatial distribution of this value is taken from the accompanying dataset (provided by Ocean Wealth) and is expressed as USD spared per coastal grid cell.

4. Spatial alignment

Each economic feature (reef polygon centroid or fisheries/coastal point) is matched to the nearest coral-cover monitoring site. Economic points beyond 5° from a reef survey site (X% of the economic datasets) are excluded from the analysis.

While the majority of the economic datasets (X%) lie within 1° (~111km) of a coral cover projection datapoint, the remaining X% for which Reef Check surveys are remote motivates additional targeted survey sampling in order to increase the representiveness of the data.

For smooth spatial visualisation on the Map without changing country aggregates, dense point layers are aggregated by mean onto a 0.1° grid.

5. Economic depreciation models

Having mapped economic value to reef surveys and projected coral cover at those points, we need relationships between cover change and value change — reflecting, for example, reefs becoming less attractive to tourists or providing less fish habitat as they degrade.

These are deliberate oversimplifications. Regional, temporal, and cultural variation is large, but we apply one functional form per sector globally because finer-scaled estimates are not supported by the data. The results are intended as first-order, discussion-ready projections of how reef degradation may affect currently reef-derived economic services.

The interactive panels below show how each sector translates coral cover change into remaining economic value. Compound and tipping-point curves appear under tourism as cross-sector sensitivity scenarios.

Tourism — Chen et al. depreciation

Chen et al. (2015) tourism elasticity: 3.81% value loss per 1% relative coral loss (Vrem = V0 × max(0, 1 + 3.807 × ΔC/C0)). The compound and tipping-point panels are sensitivity scenarios applied identically across all sectors in the dashboard.

35%

Fisheries — habitat quality model

Fisheries value scales with coral cover via a habitat-quality multiplier on Sea Around Us country baselines:

m = α + (1 − α)(Cfinal/Cbaseline),  Vrem = V0 × m

α is the habitat floor: the minimum share of fisheries value retained when coral cover reaches zero. The dashboard uses α = 0.4; use the slider to explore other values. At α = 0, no value remains at zero cover; at α = 1, cover has no effect.

0.4

Coastal protection — cover-proportional loss

Coastal protection value scales 1:1 with relative coral cover change (Chen et al., 2015):

Vrem = V0 × (1 + ΔC/C0)

Uses the same C0 as the tourism panel above.

6. Cumulative impact over time

Endpoint losses (2050 / 2100) understate long-run harm because value is lost in every intervening year. We construct coral cover trajectories from a 2013 baseline to each scenario endpoint (linear or exponential interpolation), then compute year-by-year remaining value and opportunity cost.

  • Annual loss — year-over-year decline in remaining value.
  • Cumulative loss — sum of annual opportunity cost (V0 − Vt) discounted at 0% by default.
  • Tipping-point models are evaluated per site so reefs with higher C0 cross the collapse threshold later.

7. Country aggregation & GDP context

Site-level original_value, remaining_value, and value_loss are summed by country (ISO A3). Loss as a share of national GDP uses World Bank GDP (constant USD) for the reference year.

Country charts support annual endpoint loss and cumulative loss scenarios. The “all datasets” view sums tourism, fisheries, and coastal protection — note units differ by layer but aggregates are shown for comparative ranking.

8. Limitations, uncertainty, & next steps

  • Coral projections inherit structural and parameter uncertainty from the beta regression; RCP 8.5 is a high-emissions sensitivity, not a forecast.
  • Nearest-neighbour alignment can mismatch reef ecology and economic activity where monitoring sites are sparse.
  • Tourism elasticities follow Chen et al. (2015) meta-analysis; fisheries now use SAU country baselines and a habitat floor — both are simplifying assumptions.
  • SAU values end in 2019; “Others” functional-group buckets may hide taxonomic detail at low API limit.
  • Coastal protection and tourism layers are static baselines — they do not yet respond dynamically to socio-economic change independent of coral cover.
  • Compound and tipping-point models illustrate non-linear sensitivities; they are not calibrated independently to reef econometric data.
